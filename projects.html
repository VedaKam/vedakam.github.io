Spatial Co-Expression Prediction (MHCA)

Overview

Core thesis project in the De Vlaminck Lab at Cornell: a co-expression prediction model using Borzoi gene embeddings (4096 tokens × 1536 dimensions) fed through bidirectional cross-attention, predicting pairwise co-expression across 16 spatial RNA tracks from mouse embryo development (E9.5–E16.5).

Results

Achieved Pearson R≈0.674 within-chunk and R≈0.548 in zero-shot generalization. The most scientifically generative finding was a structured high-error gene module pattern, hypothesized to involve epigenetic regulation — motivating a three-part thesis structure: (1) mechanistic characterization of the generalization gap via attention attribution, (2) biological annotation of difficult-to-predict gene modules against ENCODE chromatin state data, and (3) multimodal intervention using Borzoi-predicted ATAC track outputs as auxiliary input.

Glioma Segmentation — Safety-Focused Design

Overview

An ML system for glioma segmentation built around a deliberate safety choice: using a retrieval-based approach for generating medical summaries, rather than a generative language model, to avoid hallucination risk in safety-critical clinical outputs. Co-authored with Antonio Garces.

Why Retrieval, Not Generation

Generative summarization is prone to hallucinating clinically plausible but incorrect details — an unacceptable failure mode in a medical context. A retrieval-based design constrains outputs to grounded, verifiable source content, trading some fluency for interpretability and auditability.

Related Work

This project connects to a second, in-progress effort: a clinician-in-the-loop benchmarking engine for medical AI validation, informed by published research on scalable oversight and alignment evaluation.

Stealth Health Tech

Project Overview

This is an ongoing research and engineering project focused on the benchmarking and evaluation of AI systems in healthcare workflows. The work centers on how AI agents behave under realistic clinical constraints, rather than on deploying end-user products.

Technical Focus

The project investigates methods for evaluating AI-assisted clinical tasks, including agent reliability, failure modes, and observability. Emphasis is placed on designing evaluation frameworks that make model behavior auditable and defensible in high-stakes settings.

Current Status

The project is in active development and remains private while core evaluation infrastructure and experimental design are being refined. Select components and insights may be shared publicly once the framework reaches a stable and reproducible state.

Motivation

Many healthcare AI systems are assessed primarily on performance metrics that do not capture real-world failure modes. This project aims to bridge that gap by focusing on evaluation, interpretability, and system-level accountability rather than model outputs alone.

Cornell DEBUT — SteadyStride

Leadership & Scope

Served as Team Lead for Cornell DEBUT, coordinating a 70-member cross-functional organization spanning four R&D teams, economics, and operations. I set technical direction, managed inter-team dependencies, and acted as the primary interface for external stakeholders, including clinicians, manufacturers, and grant providers.

Problem & Role

Following market discovery identifying gait instability driven by hand tremors, I was recruited to lead the alpha prototype design. I owned system-level decisions across mechanical modeling, prototyping, testing methodology, and external presentation.

Mechanical Modeling & Simulation

Modeled a tuned-mass damper system using a 300+ line MATLAB simulation, solving the governing ODEs of Parkinsonian tremor response. The simulation explored discrete mass–spring configurations to minimize residual displacement and informed final component selection.

Prototype Design & Assembly

Designed the damper assembly and stabilizing base in Fusion360, optimizing for tremor dampening, ground contact, and manufacturability. Components were sourced from McMaster-Carr and fabricated via FDM printing, resulting in a functional alpha prototype integrated with a standard cane.

Clinical Testing (IRB-Approved)

Led IRB approval and clinical testing at SUNY Cortland’s Biomechanics Lab. Developed custom accelerometer-based testing equipment and analyzed over 80,000 data points in MATLAB. Patient trials demonstrated a ~12–15% reduction in hand tremor amplitude for the majority of participants.

Productization & External Presentation

Transitioned the research prototype into a minimum viable product by redesigning components with DFM and supply-chain constraints in mind. Led the investor pitch deck and presentation for the Medtronic × BMES Student Design Competition, earning 1st Place ($2,500).

Coord — Product Design

Coord — Collaborative Workspace for Engineering Teams

Coord is a research-driven product design project focused on centralizing coordination for Cornell Engineering project teams. The core problem identified through user research was fragmentation across tools (Slack, spreadsheets, notes), which led to loss of visibility, inefficient task delegation, and increased cognitive load for team leads.

Problem Framing & User Research

Conducted interviews, surveys, and usability testing with engineering project team members and leads. Research revealed that team leads spend disproportionate time coordinating logistics rather than leading technical work. This informed a primary persona (“Amy”), whose goals emphasized rapid situational awareness, task delegation, and workload management across subteams.

System Design & Information Architecture

Designed the product around a clear hierarchy from global overview to subteam-level actions, minimizing cognitive overload while preserving visibility. Core system components include a Home Dashboard for progress health checks, a Tasks module for delegation and tracking, a Gantt-style Timeline for dependency visualization, and a Pulse system for monitoring team capacity and constraints.

Key Design Decisions

Interface and interaction decisions were grounded in usability principles such as visibility of system status, match between system and real-world workflows, and user control. Examples include color-coded progress indicators, editable subteam structures, Gantt-based timelines for parallel task scanning, and workload-focused pulse inputs rather than abstract mood tracking.

Prototyping & Evaluation

Built a high-fidelity interactive prototype in Figma to evaluate navigation clarity, information hierarchy, and task flow. User testing informed refinements in terminology, navigation consistency, and visual affordances for interactivity. Features such as AI-driven task recommendations were intentionally scoped as decision-support tools rather than automated actions.

Outcome

The final prototype demonstrates a cohesive coordination system designed to reduce friction, improve awareness across subteams, and support better decision-making under real-world constraints. This project strengthened my approach to designing systems that balance usability, scalability, and interpretability.

Independent Research

I’ve pursued independent, end-to-end research projects focused on applying machine learning, sensing systems, and computational biology to real-world healthcare and urban challenges. These projects emphasize system design, data pipelines, and interpretable outputs rather than isolated models.

The system estimates AQI levels, tracks pollutant exposure trends, and generates user-facing recommendations using statistical modeling.

  • Applied regression models for trend analysis and recommendations

Precision Medicine for Kidney Disease (Computational Genomics)

Developed an automated, cloud-based bioinformatics pipeline for identifying potential microRNA biomarkers for chronic kidney disease. The system abstracts complex genomics workflows into an accessible, reproducible platform.

  • AWS-based workflow for large-scale genomic data
  • Genome alignment and variant calling using Bowtie, SAMtools, GATK
  • Generated VCF outputs as inputs for downstream ML-driven biomarker discovery

First Hands-On Neural Network (Medical Imaging)

An early, self-directed learning project: adapted PyTorch's CIFAR-10 tutorial to a real dataset, fine-tuning a DenseNet-121 to classify skin lesion images from HAM10000. Included lesion-level train/val/test splitting to avoid data leakage between duplicate images, and experimentation with class-imbalance handling. Not a production system — this is where the fundamentals of CNNs and transfer learning were learned hands-on.

Collectively, these projects reflect my interest in building interpretable, production-aware ML systems that bridge raw data, modeling, and real-world decision-making.
